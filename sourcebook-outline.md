# Middle-earth in the First and Second Ages

*Working chapter outline for a Rolemaster Second Edition campaign sourcebook*

The assembled book is available as an [illustrated reading edition](book/middle-earth-in-the-first-and-second-ages.pdf) and an [editable manuscript](book/middle-earth-in-the-first-and-second-ages.md). This outline records the earlier development plan. The manuscript now has two parts, setting and tone followed by procedures for play. See the [research notebook](book/research-notes.md) for unresolved matters and the [register of adopted choices](book/middle-earth-in-the-first-and-second-ages.md#register-of-campaign-choices) for the decisions in the reading edition.

The book is for a GM who knows RM2 and wants to run either Age. Tolkien supplies the setting and tone. MERP supplies a body of game material to draw on. RM2 supplies the rules at the table. Setting entries should distinguish Tolkien’s accounts, MERP interpretations, and inventions for this campaign.

At the outline stage, a chapter can begin with a passage and a question worth keeping. Possible answers belong alongside it while they are fresh. When we return to an idea, the encounter gives us something concrete to judge it by: what could the player notice, what could they choose, and how would that choice matter?

## Part I. Choosing a time and place

[From the Music to the Last Alliance](history-of-the-two-ages.md) introduces both Ages to first-time players. For a campaign built around discovery, give players the history up to their starting date. Use the [gazetteer of kingdoms and regions](gazetteer-kingdoms-and-regions.md) with Chapters 2 and 3 to choose a homeland, its neighbours, and the powers a traveler would encounter at that date.

Both Ages support campaigns beginning in [Rhûn](gazetteer-kingdoms-and-regions.md#rhûn-and-the-eastern-lands) or [Harad, Umbar, and Khand](gazetteer-kingdoms-and-regions.md#harad-and-the-southern-lands). Give eastern and southern protagonists local obligations, histories, and neighbours they can call on before any encounter with a western power.

**1. Running a heroic age.** Two encounters frame the book. Fingolfin wounds Morgoth permanently and loses their duel. What would give a player the chance to accomplish something as consequential in defeat? In Eregion, what could an Elf notice about Annatar that would give them reason to distrust him, and what might a friend of his make of the same evidence?

**2. The First Age: Beleriand through the wars.** Offer playable moments in the long watch on Angband, the aftermath of the Battle of Sudden Flame, and the years when the great realms fall. For each, show who holds the roads, where news travels, which communities offer shelter, and what an individual adventurer could change. Give the Edain, the Dwarves of the Blue Mountains, and the people living between great strongholds a place alongside the Elven courts.

**3. The Second Age: Eregion, Númenor, and the lands between.** Give distinct campaign windows for Khazad-dûm and the rise of Eregion, Annatar and the work of the smiths, and the later spread of Númenórean dominion and Sauron’s influence. A timeline should tell the GM which havens, realms, and Rings exist at the chosen date. An optional entry could place early hobbit communities along the Anduin, identified as a campaign conjecture.

## Part II. People and professions

**4. Origins and heroic stature.** Compare an Elf who remembers the Trees, an Elf born in Middle-earth, a Dwarf trained in a great workshop, and a Númenórean raised in a mainland haven. What could each do at the beginning of play? Does native spiritual stature tend to differ between Elven generations? This is a question about gifts at birth. Elven fading is a separate subject. Personal gifts and access to particular teachers are two possible ways to express unequal backgrounds within RM2.

**5. Professions in Middle-earth.** Adapt selected RM2 professions: ranger, scout or thief, soldier or warrior, bard, seer, astrologer, healer, enchanter, and cleric. Give each a recognizable occupation, routes to training, a review of skill costs and spell access, and an example from each Age where appropriate. Explore devotion to individual Valar and service to Morgoth. Leave player allegiance open.

## Part III. Rules for encounters and works of power

**6. The Seen and the Unseen.** Three Riders withdraw before Glorfindel. What should a Rider fear on recognizing Galadriel, and how could the player perceive that fear? One possibility is a fear check for the Rider. Another is for the GM to have him keep his distance until companions arrive or his orders compel him to approach. Annatar’s reception in Eregion raises a different question: what can spiritual insight tell an observer about a stranger’s intentions?

**Annatar in the workshop.** In this campaign Sauron conducts himself among the smiths as an Elf would. His assumed body handles a tool as readily as Celebrimbor’s, and his pleasure in a difficult piece of work can be genuine. In one account he claims to have been sent by the Valar. A smith might readily believe that someone with such knowledge had studied under Aulë. Galadriel, who remembers Valinor, has grounds to question who sent him. Tolkien treats her recollection of Aulë’s household as evidence with limits. [Letter 200, on Sauron’s embodied form](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/59d68c02c534a5079b390c38/t/5c2d3e3f575d1f37b45d4750/1546468936692/the_letters_of_j.rrtolkien.pdf) and [*Unfinished Tales*, “The History of Galadriel and Celeborn,” including note 7](https://ebookscape.com/middle-earth/unfinished-tales-j-r-r-tolkien/)

**What did the stranger claim?** In the Eregion account he claims the Valar sent him to Middle-earth to help the Elves, or gave him orders to remain there for that purpose. The latter story would require no recent voyage from Aman. A separate note gives him the names *Artano* (high-smith) or *Aulendil* (one devoted to Aulë). No apprenticeship under a named teacher is described. The *Valaquenta* says that Sauron himself had belonged to the Maiar of Aulë and retained their lore. His finest work could therefore stand up to an expert’s scrutiny. [*Unfinished Tales*, “The History of Galadriel and Celeborn,” note 7](https://ebookscape.com/middle-earth/unfinished-tales-j-r-r-tolkien/) and [*The Silmarillion*, “Valaquenta”](https://edspace.american.edu/justinjacobs/wp-content/uploads/sites/986/2025/01/Silmarillion.pdf)

**Who had grounds to doubt him?** In the note about Aulendil, Galadriel says he was absent from Aulë’s following in Valinor as she knew it. The note leaves room for service to Aulë long before her time. Gil-galad and Elrond both distrusted his fair appearance, refused him entry to Lindon, and sent warnings. Tolkien gives no particular incident that first alerted either of them. Gil-galad had already warned Tar-Meneldur that a servant of Morgoth was stirring in the East. That gives a GM a reason for caution without making the king privy to Annatar’s identity. [*The Silmarillion*, “Of the Rings of Power and the Third Age”](https://martijn.coppoolse.com/books/part/1233/of-the-rings-of-power-and-the-third-age) and [*Unfinished Tales*, “Aldarion and Erendis” and “The History of Galadriel and Celeborn”](https://ebookscape.com/middle-earth/unfinished-tales-j-r-r-tolkien/)

**What would it mean to equal Fëanor?** Tolkien gives Celebrimbor the ambition to rival his grandfather in both skill and fame. For this campaign, let that ambition guide his own designs. Fëanor preserved the light of the Trees in jewels that survived the Trees themselves. Could Celebrimbor make something that would preserve a beloved place while its people still lived there? As he masters what Annatar teaches, the Three become his chance to make a work he can claim as his own. Tolkien says Celebrimbor made the Three alone, yet their making still drew on an art Sauron had taught. Celebrimbor’s reason for undertaking that work unaided is our interpretation. [*Unfinished Tales*, “The History of Galadriel and Celeborn”](https://ebookscape.com/middle-earth/unfinished-tales-j-r-r-tolkien/) and [*The Silmarillion*, “Of the Rings of Power and the Third Age”](https://martijn.coppoolse.com/books/part/1233/of-the-rings-of-power-and-the-third-age)

One possible scene begins when Galadriel asks Annatar who entrusted him with this work. Before he can speak, Celebrimbor answers for him. He shows her a piece he had spent years trying to make and explains exactly where Annatar helped him. For the first time he can see how to bring a design worthy of his grandfather to completion. A player might share his delight and still remember, weeks later, that Annatar never answered the question.

For this campaign, Celebrimbor sees what he wants to see. In private he admits to Galadriel that she has grounds for doubt. He agrees to send an inquiry to Lindon. When the apprentice asks whether to lay out Annatar’s tools in the morning, he says yes. To send his guest away on suspicion might cost him the one teacher who can help him equal Fëanor. One more day at the forge seems a small thing to ask.

**7. Embodiment, wounds, and defeat.** Read Fingolfin’s duel alongside Huan and Lúthien’s encounter with Sauron. What makes a body vulnerable? What makes a wound endure? One candidate is to resolve the blow with an ordinary RM2 critical, then consider what freedom the being has to relinquish its body. Which parts of either encounter would that explain, and where would the GM still need guidance?

**8. The kinds of magic in Middle-earth.** [Chapter 8 working notes](chapter-08-kinds-of-magic.md) survey the different things Tolkien’s characters call magic, from spells over buried gold to the domination of the Nine. Begin with the deed and its circumstances. Which part belongs to the maker, the art they learned, the thing they made, or the will of another being? A Dwarven forge-song lets us explore what an outsider might learn by listening and what still has to be learned at the anvil. The campaign gives such traditions a wider place in the two Ages; their rules and limits remain open.

**9. Spell lists for the professions.** Supply original, playable RM2 lists with levels, effects, limits, and examples. Draft candidates include forge-songs for enchanters, lays of remembrance for bards, healing songs, wards for journeys, arts of discernment, and star-lore for astrologers. Give a seer a way to discover a useful clue while leaving its meaning for the player to work out. Index the finished lists by profession as well as by tradition.

**10. Great works, the Rings, and the Marring of Arda.** What can a maker spend in one act of creation that rest and practice will never replenish? Fëanor’s many inventions and his once-only Silmarils, Sauron’s investment in the One, and Morgoth’s dispersal of power into Arda give different accounts of the cost. An idea to revisit: a Dwarven smith binds her gift for hearing flaws in stone into a warning bell for her hall. Would giving the whole community the benefit of her gift make its loss a choice a player might willingly make? The Nine pose a related question about the cost to the recipient of a gift.

## Part IV. Campaigns in play

**11. Running a world the players can investigate.** Give the GM a practical way to record what happened, who saw it, what each witness believes, and what happens if nobody intervenes. Show how travel, craft, loyalties, and rumor carry evidence. Include ways to run one protagonist with occasional allies, or a party, while preserving the same dangers.

**12. First Age adventure: The Bell at the Crossing.** After the breaking of the Siege of Angband, refugees need a crossing watched by a small garrison. A Dwarf who helped build it hears a change in its warning bell; a captain believes the smith is delaying the retreat to save family possessions. The truth can be discovered by examining the stonework, learning the bell’s song, or tracing who last crossed. The characters must decide whom to move, whom to trust, and how long to stay. Demonstrates craft, cultural knowledge, and the cost of injury under pressure.

**13. Second Age adventure: A Gift at the Watergate.** An Elven smith offers to renew a river town’s flood warnings with a device learned in Eregion. Its first warning saves lives. A ferrymaster later notices that somebody upriver knows which channels the town has closed. The GM gets the device’s actual workings, the maker’s intentions, and several routes to the truth. The characters decide what to do with a gift their neighbors now rely on. Annatar’s influence can enter through an apprentice’s perfectly sincere account of his teaching.

**14. Questions the histories leave open.** What does a GM actually need to decide for an encounter to work? For each question, identify what Tolkien wrote, where his accounts differ, and what remains unknown. Offer possible rulings with an example of what each would change in play. Sometimes all a GM needs to settle is what a particular witness believes.

- **Where are the hobbits?** Their earliest tales recall the upper Vales of Anduin, but give us no secure First or Second Age address. A few river households are one possibility for the Second Age. Which neighbors would know them by name, and what would those neighbors call them? For the First Age even their whereabouts can remain an open entry on the GM’s map. [The Fellowship of the Ring, Prologue, “Concerning Hobbits”](https://www.mrsmuellersworld.com/uploads/1/3/0/5/13054185/tolkien_-_fellowship_of_the_ring.pdf)
- **What happened to the Entwives?** Treebeard remembers their gardens. In Letter 144 Tolkien suspected the Entwives perished when Sauron burned their lands in the War of the Last Alliance. An earlier Second Age adventure could let a player meet an Entwife at work and learn why neighboring farmers seek her advice. A later one might trace a pear variety from a ruined orchard to a valley full of its descendants. How much does that tell anyone about the gardener? [The Two Towers, “Treebeard”; Letter 144](https://cs.au.dk/~clemens/au15572/tolkien/entwives.html)
- **Who is Tom Bombadil?** Tolkien describes him in Letter 144 as a deliberate enigma. For play, the useful open questions include where someone might meet him in either Age, what names local people have for him, and what might move him to intervene. Two travelers could disagree about who sang them safely home and still remember the same verse. [Letter 144, to Naomi Mitchison](https://time.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/12/the_letters_of_j.rrtolkien.pdf)

### Where did Orcs come from? How free are they?

**Origin.** The published *Silmarillion* gives the belief of the wise of Eressëa that Melkor bred Orcs from captive Elves. Tolkien also explored a human origin. A difficulty runs through his revisions. Melkor can corrupt living beings, but only Eru can give a creature an independent will. A GM who adopts Elvish descent gives the disappearances near Cuiviénen a terrible significance for Elves who remember them. A GM who adopts human descent must consider when Men awoke if the campaign reaches back to the earliest Orc wars. A campaign can also preserve both accounts as traditions. The GM can decide who passed each one down and why a character trusts that witness. [*The Silmarillion*, “Of the Coming of the Elves and the Captivity of Melkor”](https://tolkien.ro/of-the-origins-of-the-orcs/) and [*Morgoth’s Ring*, “Myths Transformed,” texts VIII–X](https://tolkiendiscussion.blogspot.com/2011/04/origins-of-orcs-reading-material.html)

**Will and responsibility.** There is firmer evidence that Orcs can make choices. In one Second Age note, eastern Orcs laugh at Sauron while he wears his fair form. Their contempt establishes that he has to win or force their obedience. It says little about how they treat anyone in their own power. In a late essay Tolkien describes Orcs as possessing “independent wills” and says the Wise taught that an Orc who surrendered and asked for mercy should receive it, even at a cost. In draft Letter 153 he draws back from calling Orcs irredeemable. He leaves the ultimate fate of an individual Orc open. [*The Nature of Middle-earth*, “Note on the Delay of Gil-galad and the Númenóreans”](https://tolkiengateway.net/wiki/Eastern_Orcs), [*Morgoth’s Ring*, “Myths Transformed,” text X](https://tolkiendiscussion.blogspot.com/2011/04/origins-of-orcs-reading-material.html), and [Letter 153, draft to Peter Hastings](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/59d68c02c534a5079b390c38/t/5c2d3e3f575d1f37b45d4750/1546468936692/the_letters_of_j.rrtolkien.pdf)

**A premise for the author’s own campaign.** No Orc chose what Morgoth did to their ancestors. Each has the capacity to choose differently, however costly that choice might be. Other GMs can decide how far a master's immediate presence constrains an Orc, what becomes possible beyond his reach, and whether the players ever witness a change of heart. Any of these campaigns can leave the fate of Orcs after death a mystery.

**At the table.** In a Second Age adventure, an Orc guide accepts payment to lead a fugitive to a ford. Alone on the bank, she can keep her word at little risk. If her captain arrives first, the same act may cost her a home among her people. Decide beforehand what she values, what her captain can discover, and what happened the last time someone disobeyed. A player who learns those things has grounds to judge her offer. Keeping one promise can matter to both of them even if they part as enemies.

### Could Galadriel have sailed West after the War of Wrath?

**The sourcebook’s Galadriel.** At Alqualondë she defended the Teleri, her mother’s people. She was innocent of the Kinslaying. When her father Finarfin turned back, she continued into exile with her kin and accepted that the Doom of the Noldor fell on her too. After Beleriand fell she stayed in Middle-earth, believing the Doom still lay on her. When Frodo offers her the One Ring and she speaks of going West, our campaign reads her words as the lifting of that ancient burden. [*Unfinished Tales*, “The History of Galadriel and Celeborn”](https://ebookscape.com/middle-earth/unfinished-tales-j-r-r-tolkien/) and [*The Fellowship of the Ring*, “The Mirror of Galadriel”](https://www.teawithtolkien.com/blog/lotr-2026-6)

**What did the Valar offer her?** In “The Shibboleth of Fëanor,” the account that explicitly places her among the defenders of her Telerin kin, the Valar offer pardon to those who fought Morgoth. Galadriel could have accepted and returned. She refuses, and Tolkien gives pride as her motive. Letter 320 likewise says she refused forgiveness or permission to return and connects her eventual pardon to her refusal of the Ring. Letter 297 gives a different account. The general leave to return excluded a few leaders of the rebellion, including Galadriel. Her personal ban was eventually lifted, above all because she refused the Ring. The Doom pronounced upon the departing Noldor and Galadriel’s own permission to sail at the end of the Age are distinct questions. [“The Shibboleth of Fëanor,” excerpted in *Unfinished Tales*](https://ebookscape.com/middle-earth/unfinished-tales-j-r-r-tolkien/) and [*Letters*, nos. 297 and 320](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/59d68c02c534a5079b390c38/t/5c2d3e3f575d1f37b45d4750/1546468936692/the_letters_of_j.rrtolkien.pdf)

**For the GM.** The offer of pardon can remain an open choice. Imagine a survivor of the War of Wrath asking Galadriel to sail with her. In one telling Galadriel must explain why she will stay when she could go home. In the other she must say farewell to someone whose welcome she cannot share. Either memory could still matter to her when the smiths of Eregion speak of preserving the things they love.

**Still to choose for Eregion.** One account gives Galadriel and Celeborn authority there until Sauron persuades the smiths to seize power. Galadriel crosses Khazad-dûm into Lórinand and Celeborn stays behind. That history gives a player trying to warn Celebrimbor a different task before and after the revolt. The much later account in which Galadriel and Celeborn sail their own ship from Alqualondë also changes where they first met. Adopting that voyage would require revisiting the sourcebook’s choice that she went into exile with her kin. [*Unfinished Tales*, “The History of Galadriel and Celeborn”](https://ebookscape.com/middle-earth/unfinished-tales-j-r-r-tolkien/)

## Part V. Creatures and crafts of the two Ages

**15. The forces of darkness.** [Encounter notes](forces-of-darkness.md) cover Orcs, trolls, giants, drakes and dragons, Balrogs, werewolves, vampires, wights, wraiths, and the possible ancestors of the winged beasts. For each, ask what a witness can observe, who commands it, and when it belongs in the history. Draugluin trying to get home to his master can make a different adventure from a wolf hunting for food.

**16. What the peoples of the two Ages could make.** [Craft and technology notes](crafts-and-technology.md) trace local manufacture, trade, public works, and inherited devices. Enchantments belong here when they change what a builder or a kingdom can do. The binding on Sauron’s captured tower, Narvi and Celebrimbor’s gate, and the foundations of Barad-dûr give three different answers about who can use, maintain, or undo such a work.

**Appendices.** A chapter-by-chapter RM2 rules-change index; MERP cross-references; parallel chronologies with textual variants flagged; a profession and spell-list locator; and an annotated reading guide keyed to the encounters discussed. Both sample adventures are original campaign material.
